About the play

Plot, the adaptation, and how to use this packet.

When and where

  • Written: around 1601 to 1602, and already in the 's repertoire by early February of 1602.
    • We know this mostly from a reconstruction. The play isn't on a 1598 list of Shakespeare's plays, and the timeframe of the references and the words he uses point to these years (Internet Shakespeare Editions).
    • That puts it around the same time as Hamlet, though we'll never be able to say officially which came first.
  • First recorded performance: February 2, 1602, on , the formal end of the Christmas season. John Manningham, a law student at , where that performance took place, wrote about it in his diary.
  • Setting: Illyria, an exotic and romantic-feeling place. In our version, Illyria exists in the modern-day Florida Keys.

Shakespeare

  • Lived: around 1564 to 1616.
  • Where this play falls: around the middle of his career, after Much Ado About Nothing and As You Like It but before the great tragedies like Macbeth and King Lear, and around the same time as Hamlet (Encyclopedia.com).
  • First printed: in the 1623 , with an unusually clean text compared to other Folio texts (Bell Shakespeare).

Language

  • Early modern English (roughly 1500 to 1750) resembles our current English closely.
  • What may trip us up: spelling, punctuation, slang, and pronunciation. Modern accents don't correspond to the early modern dialect's sound system (David Crystal).
  • Verse and prose: lower-status characters speak more in prose, higher-rank characters in . Viola's speech also shifts when she speaks with Olivia.
  • Try this: pay attention to when your character speaks in verse versus prose, and consider what that might mean for that moment in the text. Why did they transition to verse for that statement?

Themes

Gender, disguise, desire

  • On stage: by convention, women weren't allowed to act on the commercial stage, so boys and young men played all the female roles. Viola is a boy playing a woman who disguises herself as a boy (Project MUSE).
  • A motif: the cross-dressed heroine who provokes female desire is an Italian theatre motif. English playwrights keep it but tone down the charge of it.
  • Offstage: women in men's clothes could be punished very harshly at this time. Think dunking in the Thames.

Class and social climbing

  • Malvolio, Olivia's steward, imagines himself in a variety of ways as Olivia's husband, and therefore her ruler or better.
  • Dress: (laws restricting clothing in public) restricted certain fabrics, colors, and styles to rank and gender.

Grief, melancholy, and madness

  • Renaissance melancholy was treated as an illness, (study guide on madness and folly).
  • Orsino: lethargic, drawn to music and poetry, and fond of his own sorrow as proof of the depth of his feeling.
  • Olivia: seven years of mourning, which is exaggerated mourning.

Marriage

  • Consent and family: medieval marriages were often arranged, with mutual consent a desirable outcome. By the early modern era, Puritans were concerned about consent and coerced marriages.
  • Who arranged them: gentry and aristocracy, to secure advantage and alliances. That was uncommon outside the higher-ranked population, and scholars disagree about marriage among the lower classes.
  • Age: most people married in their mid to late twenties, and the legal age of consent may have been as low as 12 for girls and 14 for boys.
  • More: Folger on courtship and marriage, Agecroft Hall, and Shakespeare, Law, and Marriage (Cambridge).

The Twelfth Night celebration

  • The holiday: Twelfth Night is the eve of Epiphany (January 5), and the Twelfth Night Feast (January 6) closed out the period of Christmas revels.
  • The play: this play kind of has nothing to do with the holiday itself, date-wise, and the text never actually mentions it. We can speculate that Shakespeare liked the title as a way to invoke a festive occasion of class collapse under , rather than the very specific occasion of the Twelfth Night festival.
  • The customs: a bunch of traditions come into play. The Lord of Misrule orchestrates the revels and makes sure that class conventions and rules collapse and people have a grand time without concern for the conventions of daily life. Everyone could party together to some extent, and servants and masters might even swap roles for a while for fun.

The Lord of Misrule, and Feste

  • Feste is an allowed fool, and generally considered the Lord of Misrule because he's the only person generally allowed to criticize his superiors without endangering his position or life. That makes him an outlet for the lower-class people in the play.
  • But Feste is always allowed this freedom within the confines of his job.
  • Sir Toby is another argument for the Lord of Misrule, since he orchestrates the foolishness, revelry, and mischief around him. Along with his Lady of Misrule, Maria, he may be the strongest candidate (this article).

Carnivalesque and festival politics

  • How we read festive comedy: it's often evaluated on its reversals, its subversion of authority, and its disguise and festival contexts.
  • Bakhtin: we owe a lot of the scholarship on festival politics to and his concept of (Rabelais and His World). Carnival is a liminal time and space in which we can ignore the norms of real life and the position of our class and do some real mischief and damage.
  • Permitted release: it's safe even for the governing class, because when you leave the carnival, you go back to who you are and what is expected of you.
  • Think about: the Lord of Misrule, and authentic rebellion versus permitted release. Discussions of class and resistance in this play ultimately land on our reading of it. Are the characters engaging, as Shakespeare perhaps indicates in his title, in a temporary, permitted form of release that will dissipate at the end of the festival period, or in a rebellion that will stay with them beyond the "carnival"?
  • At the end of the play, misrule and romance give way to the everyday world, and Malvolio swears revenge. The usual rules and status return.
  • Modern-day parallels: we'll need to discuss at the end who goes back to the old rules and who gets to keep having fun, and ultimately, who pays for the fun that is had in this revelry period?
