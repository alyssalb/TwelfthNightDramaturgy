Shakespeare

  • Lived: around 1564 to 1616.
  • Where this play falls: around the middle of his career, after Much Ado About Nothing and As You Like It but before the great tragedies like Macbeth and King Lear, and around the same time as Hamlet (Encyclopedia.com).

Shakespeare’s Language and Scansion

The Basics

Shakespeare writes in a poetic meter called “iambic pentameter”. This means that lines written in verse (poetry) in his plays are composed of:

  • Iamb: a two-syllable pair, usually the first unstressed and the second stressed (marked as u /).
  • Pentameter: each line contains 5 iambs, so usually 10 syllables.

Verse like this can be rhymed or Blank Verse (unrhymed but still in iambic pentameter). He also writes in prose (regular every day language).

Why mark scansion in your script?

Shakespeare writes in a particular rhythm, which his audience would have been used to. We need to notice each time he breaks from that established pattern, like when a line is shorter or longer than the expected 10 syllables, if the stress does or doesn’t fall in the expected syllable, if a character switches between prose and verse, etc.

Marking the pattern helps you visualize the expected rhythm and more clearly notice when the text deviates from it, so you can figure out why it does that in that moment. It also helps to put tricky lines into your own words as you read them, so you know what your character is trying to communicate.

We will break the patterns and rules for a variety of reasons during this production process. But knowing the rule helps us ensure we break it on purpose and stay true to the emotional and thematic context of the original script. Some reasons Shakespeare might break from an established pattern:

  • Strong feelings, which might break a speaker from their rhythm or move them in or out of verse.
  • Short lines or caesuras (pauses in the middle of lines), which help to push speech forward or change thoughts mid-line.
  • A feminine ending (an extra 11th unstressed syllable at the end of a line), which softens the landing, indicating perhaps gentleness, sweetness, uncertainty, or trailing off.
  • Moving between verse and prose, which can indicate a shift in status (of the speaker or the person they are talking to), in feeling, or in who is being addressed.

Verse and prose in Twelfth Night

  • Orsino talks about love mostly in verse. Malvolio mostly speaks in prose, even when he talks about Olivia.
  • Toby and Andrew are gentlemen, but they mostly speak in prose because they keep the company of the servants in the play who speak that way.
  • Viola sometimes speaks in prose (such as when Maria is in the room) and moves to verse when she’s alone with Olivia. Olivia starts in prose with Cesario and moves into verse as she starts to court them.
  • When Orsino, Viola, and Olivia get really passionate, they sometimes move into rhymed verse for emphasis.

How to mark iambic pentameter verse in your script

Step 1: Is this line verse or prose?

Look at the page. Verse lines usually begin with a capital letter and stop short of the right margin. Prose generally runs the full length of the page (margin to margin). Pairs of rhyming lines (couplets) are often used to highlight a specific moment or idea. Circle the line where a scene switches between these modes, and take note of who the speaker is talking to when it happens.

Step 2: Find the stresses

This is usually the tricky part! To find stresses, say the line out loud the way you would in regular speech, and tap it out on the table as you say it. The syllables that get a tap are stressed (MU-sic). Stress is partly up to interpretation, so interpret as you mark! If you are not sure, stress the word that carries more meaning. If you are really not sure, set up a time to meet with me to figure it out together!

Step 3: Mark the syllables above the line with these common marks

  • / stressed syllable
  • u unstressed syllable
  • | divides one foot from the next foot
  • || caesura, the pause or change of thought inside a line
  • (u) feminine ending, an extra unstressed syllable at the end of a line

Sometimes Shakespeare swaps in a different pattern for emphasis. The one to know for now is a trochee (/ u), which puts the stressed syllable first and the unstressed second. This is relatively common at the start of a line or after a caesura.

Examples

Twelfth Night, five regular iambs:

u/|u/|u/|u/|u/
IfMU|sicBE|theFOOD|ofLOVE|playON

Hamlet, five iambs plus a feminine ending:

u/|u/|u/|u/|u/|(u)
toBE|orNOT|toBE|thatIS|theQUES|tion

Sonnet 29, a trochee to start and a caesura after the third stressed syllable:

/u|u/|u/||u/|u/
HAPly|iTHINK|onTHEE||andTHEN|mySTATE

Step 4: Figure out what the line means

Where does the pattern break, and in what way? What is the character doing in that moment, and who are they talking to?

If the speaker switches from verse to prose (or vice versa), who are they speaking to? What changed that prompted them to switch?

Later, if you want to get more advanced

We have a few more marks that we can play with!

  • → Run on, where the thought runs on into the next line of poetry. A period or comma at the end of a line means that line is end-stopped.
  • ] [ Shared line, where the line is split between two characters that make one full verse line. Keep the rhythm continuous with no gap.
  • ^ A silent beat, which is part of the rhythm but no one speaks.
  • / / Spondee, or two stressed syllables in a row. The line slows down and has more weight.
  • u u Pyrrhic, or two unstressed syllables in a row. The line is usually quicker or hurried.

Themes

Gender, disguise, desire

  • Women weren't allowed to act on the commercial stage at the time of writing, so boys and young men played all the female roles. Viola is a boy playing a woman who disguises herself as a boy (Project MUSE).
  • The cross-dressed heroine who provokes female desire is an Italian theatre motif. English playwrights keep it but tone down the charge of it.
  • Offstage, women in men's clothes could be punished very harshly at this time. They could be punished in a variety of generally public and humiliating ways if caught committing this offense.

Class and social climbing

  • Malvolio, Olivia's steward, imagines himself in a variety of ways as Olivia's husband, and therefore her ruler or better.
  • (laws restricting clothing in public) restricted certain fabrics, colors, and styles to rank and gender.

Grief, melancholy, and madness

  • Renaissance melancholy was treated as an illness, (study guide on madness and folly).
  • Orsino is lethargic, drawn to music and poetry, and fond of his own sorrow as proof of the depth of his feeling.

Marriage

  • Now: people choose spouses based on personal interests and love.
  • Then: marriage was shaped by family expectations, social standing, and political and economic interests. Families often controlled courtship and arranged marriages to protect wealth, increase social standing, and build political alliances. For the aristocracy, marriage mattered to their country as well as their families.
  • One method of upward mobility, in very limited cases, would have been marriage, which is a crucial part of the plot of Twelfth Night. Given the historical background, marriage was mostly used to maintain or promote social stratification. In the Elizabethan era, marriage was tied to social stratification, gender issues, and courtship, and the most important thing about it was the stability it brought, especially to the aristocracy.
  • Medieval marriages were often arranged, with mutual consent being a desirable outcome but not a necessity. By the early modern era, Puritans were concerned about consent and coerced marriages.
  • Divorce was impossible, so marriage was meant for life.
  • Marriage thus carried a lot of pressure. "those who defied the conventions could suffer disgrace, disinheritance, and harsh legal penalties" (Cook 5).
  • Most people married in their mid to late twenties, and the legal age of consent may have been as low as 12 for girls and 14 for boys.

How did marriage work among the higher and lower classes?

Marriage did not necessarily reflect love. It served to confirm power, legacy, influence, and wealth.

  • Among the elite, people married earlier than in other classes so both parties could benefit from one another, maintain their status, gain wealth, and build a reputation.
    • Before marriage, individuals were practically considered "lesser citizens" (Cook 8). Marrying and consolidating status helped them appear responsible and worthy. That meant planning at a very young age, finding the right partner and family, and consolidating .
    • Marrying someone of a different standing was generally discouraged. Marriages were meant to occur between people of similar age, status, complexion, constitution, wealth, and rank, and a marriage across ranks was considered unequal and disapproved of (Cook 40).
    • Because marriage was in a sense transactional, individuals could rise or survive within their rank through it.
  • Among the lower classes marriage functioned differently. Some unmarried women had more freedom to choose their partners, though families could still arrange marriages.
    • Little information exists about the lowest social groups, because economic hardship may have prevented them from marrying at all.
    • For ambitious individuals, marrying the right partner offered a shortcut to status and power (Cook).
    • London had more men than women, which created opportunities for women of lower status, often servants, to marry their masters or other elites they worked for.

Gender roles

  • Strictly enforced, especially for women, who were prohibited even from performing onstage and punished for crossdressing in public (i.e. wearing pants). These roles also shaped the power dynamics of marriage.
  • Men:
    • Depending on men's social standing and income, most could refuse marriage.
    • They were expected to marry within their rank, never below it, because marrying down could affect their virtue and standing.
    • Unmarried men, especially heirs or sons of aristocrats, could control some of their patrimony, govern their households, and maintain a respectable identity within the commonwealth (Cook 8).
  • Women:
    • Women held far less power within marriage and were expected to maintain stability in political unions, often with little agency in choosing their husbands.
    • Their fertility, intelligence, and health determined whether they could secure a marriage, and they were expected to maintain their virginity so that their children's lineage could be traced to their biological father.
    • They were often dependent on their fathers before marriage. Marriage could give them some authority: although it "placed her under a husband's authority, she at least exercised authority over her dwelling, her children, her servants" (Cook 8).

A recurring theme in Shakespeare

  • Cook notes that Shakespeare's experiences with marriage and social mobility may reflect how marriage functioned in his time. His grandmother left an estate to his father, which allowed his father to rise in standing, though the family's later economic and property struggles signaled a decline in their fortune (Cook 8). Through his profession, Shakespeare rose again in rank, which helped his daughters marry men with respectable professions.
  • Shakespeare's plays can be read as reflections on English marriage practices, within their historical context rather than through the marriage conventions of twentieth-century spectators.
  • People familiar with the hierarchy and marriage laws of the time would recognize how improbable it was to marry across social status. Shakespeare used such marriages not only for humor but to challenge the existing hierarchy: although they may have seemed unrealistic, they suggested that marriage could cross social boundaries. As Cook says, "In dramatizing fictional marriages between men and women of unequal rank, Shakespeare hews to no single pattern. Occasionally, the difference simply seems to touch on the realities of ordinary life" (53).
  • In the play, characters abandon their duties, responsibilities, and social positions, which is why marriage is so closely connected to the theme of social class.
  • Source: the marriage section above draws on Cook's work (chapters 1 and 3).
  • Further Reading on Marriage and Courtship: Folger on courtship and marriage, Agecroft Hall, and Shakespeare, Law, and Marriage (Cambridge).

The Twelfth Night celebration

  • Twelfth Night is the eve of Epiphany (January 5), and the Twelfth Night Feast (January 6) closed out the period of Christmas revels.
  • This play kind of has nothing to do with the holiday itself, date-wise, and the text never actually mentions it. We can speculate that Shakespeare liked the title as a way to invoke a festive occasion of class collapse under , rather than the very specific occasion of the Twelfth Night festival.
  • A bunch of traditions come into play. The Lord of Misrule orchestrates the revels and makes sure that class conventions and rules collapse and people have a grand time without concern for the conventions of daily life. Everyone could party together to some extent, and servants and masters might even swap roles for a while for fun.

The Lord of Misrule, and Feste

  • Feste is an allowed fool, and generally considered the Lord of Misrule because he's the only person generally allowed to criticize his superiors without endangering his position or life. That makes him an outlet for the lower-class people in the play.
  • But Feste is always allowed this freedom within the confines of his job.
  • Sir Toby is another argument for the Lord of Misrule, since he orchestrates the foolishness, revelry, and mischief around him. Along with his Lady of Misrule, Maria, he may be the strongest candidate (this article).

Carnivalesque and festival politics

  • Festive comedy is often evaluated on its reversals, its subversion of authority, and its disguise and festival contexts.
  • We owe a lot of the scholarship on festival politics to and his concept of (Rabelais and His World). Carnival is a liminal time and space in which we can ignore the norms of real life and the position of our class, and there is a potential for real mischief and possibly even damage.
  • The carnivalesque offers a permitted release, meaning it is safe for even the governing class, because when you leave the carnival, you go back to who you are and what is expected of you.
  • The Lord of Misrule brings up questions about authentic rebellion versus permitted release. Discussions of class and resistance in this play ultimately land on our reading of it. Are the characters engaging, as Shakespeare perhaps indicates in his title, in a temporary, permitted form of release that will dissipate at the end of the festival period, or in a rebellion that will stay with them beyond the "carnival"?
  • At the end of the play, misrule and romance give way to the everyday world, and Malvolio swears revenge. The usual rules and status return.
