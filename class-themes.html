Social hierarchy in Elizabethan England

What did the hierarchy look like?

  • The clearest division was between gentlemen and non-gentlemen: aristocrats who mainly owned land or whose families had great wealth and power, versus farmers and servants who worked directly for a living. Within that division there were six subdivisions:
    1. Live-in servants, widows, the unemployed, and the aged, dependent on charity.
    2. Laborers: agricultural and urban workers who lived outside their employers' households.
    3. Farmers and tradespeople: , husbandmen, artisans, shopkeepers.
    4. Local gentlemen, usually connected with land ownership.
    5. The county elite: squires, knights, and barons.
    6. Peers: dukes, marquises, earls, viscounts, and barons.
  • Outside the hierarchy: merchants, lawyers, and religious figures were seen as outsiders and gained power and influence through money and position. Their social status roughly corresponded to the income pyramid, as Lawrence Stone explains (Stone 21).
  • A shifting standard: social standing moved away from being based solely on land ownership toward something more complex: money, profession, family background, and social recognition. There were multiple avenues for advancement, but that didn't mean someone's perceived standing automatically matched the group they were trying to join.

Was social mobility possible in the lower classes?

  • Most people were lower class: according to , 90 to 95 percent of the population (Stone 20).
  • Moving up was extremely difficult for groups 1, 2, and 3. Opportunities were limited, and agricultural labor was the main means of survival. Even mobility within their own class, through new economic opportunities or a new occupation, was limited, and moving to a different class was less common. Lower groups could still find some mobility.
  • Sponsored mobility: mobility depends on how accessible the upper classes are and how much the elites provide or control advancement. In sponsored mobility, someone in the upper class supports another's rise.
  • For the play: the mobility of desire, whether Malvolio's or anyone else's, is largely determined and controlled by the upper classes.

Marriage in Elizabethan England

  • Why it matters: marriage is an important part of the plot of Twelfth Night. Given the historical background, it was mostly used to maintain or promote social stratification. Today we talk about love, mutual values, and relationships. In the Elizabethan era, marriage was tied to social stratification, gender issues, and courtship, and the most important thing about it was the stability it brought, especially to the aristocracy.
  • Source: the marriage sections below draw on Cook's work (chapters 1 and 3).

How did marriage practices differ from today?

  • Then: marriage was shaped by family expectations, social standing, and political and economic interests. Families often controlled courtship and arranged marriages to protect wealth, increase social standing, and build political alliances. For the aristocracy, marriage mattered to their country as well as their families.
  • Now: people choose spouses based on personal interests and love.
  • For life: divorce was impossible, so marriage was meant for life.
  • The pressure: "those who defied the conventions could suffer disgrace, disinheritance, and harsh legal penalties" (Cook 5).

Gender roles

  • Strictly enforced, especially for women, who were prohibited even from performing on stage. These roles also shaped the power dynamics of marriage.
  • Men:
    • Depending on their social standing and income, most could refuse marriage.
    • They were expected to marry within their rank, never below it, because marrying down could affect their virtue and standing.
    • Unmarried men, especially heirs or sons of aristocrats, could control some of their patrimony, govern their households, and maintain a respectable identity within the commonwealth (Cook 8).
  • Women:
    • They held far less power within marriage and were expected to maintain stability in political unions, often with little agency in choosing their husbands.
    • Their fertility, intelligence, and health determined whether they could secure a marriage, and they were expected to maintain their virginity so that their children's lineage could be traced to their biological father.
    • They were often dependent on their fathers before marriage. Marriage could give them some authority: although it "placed her under a husband's authority, she at least exercised authority over her dwelling, her children, her servants" (Cook 8).

How did marriage work among the higher and lower classes?

Again, marriage did not necessarily reflect love. It served to confirm power, legacy, influence, and wealth.

  • Among the elite:
    • People married earlier than in other classes, so both parties could benefit from one another, maintain their status, gain wealth, and build a reputation.
    • Before marriage, individuals were practically considered "lesser citizens" (Cook 8). Marrying and consolidating status helped them appear responsible and worthy. That meant planning at a very young age, finding the right partner and family, and consolidating .
    • Marrying someone of a different standing was generally discouraged. Marriages were meant to occur between people of similar age, status, complexion, constitution, wealth, and rank, and a marriage across ranks was considered unequal and disapproved of (Cook 40).
    • Because marriage was in a sense transactional, individuals could rise or survive within their rank through it.
  • Among the lower classes:
    • Marriage functioned differently. Some unmarried women had more freedom to choose their partners, though families could still arrange marriages.
    • Little information exists about the lowest social groups, because economic hardship may have prevented them from marrying at all.
    • For ambitious individuals, marrying the right partner offered a shortcut to status and power (Cook).
    • London had more men than women, which created opportunities for women of lower status, often servants, to marry their masters or other elites they worked for.

A recurring theme in Shakespeare

  • His own life: Cook notes that Shakespeare's experiences with marriage and social mobility may reflect how marriage functioned in his time. His grandmother left an estate to his father, which allowed his father to rise in standing, though the family's later economic and property struggles signaled a decline in their fortune (Cook 8). Through his profession, Shakespeare rose again in rank, which helped his daughters marry men with respectable professions.
  • Reading the plays: they can be read as reflections of English marriage practices, within their historical context rather than through the marriage conventions of twentieth-century spectators.
  • Crossing boundaries: people familiar with the hierarchy and marriage laws of the time would recognize how improbable it was to marry across social status. Shakespeare used such marriages not only for humor but to challenge the existing hierarchy: although they may have seemed unrealistic, they suggested that marriage could cross social boundaries. As Cook says, "In dramatizing fictional marriages between men and women of unequal rank, Shakespeare hews to no single pattern. Occasionally, the difference simply seems to touch on the realities of ordinary life" (53).
  • In the play, characters abandon their duties, responsibilities, and social positions, which is why marriage is so closely connected to the theme of social class.

Class in contemporary Key West

Who lives in Key West today?

  • A diverse population: information on specific groups is limited, but demographic statistics suggest a racially and culturally diverse community, including Cuban, Bahamian, white, Black or African American, and other backgrounds.
  • Significant economic divisions: wealthy individuals, investors, and business owners can afford expensive properties and lifestyles, while many of the workers who sustain the island's economy struggle with the high cost of living.
  • Several distinct community groups:
Group Who they are
Conch Long-term inhabitants of Key West, usually with Bahamian and Caribbean lineage dating back to the nineteenth century.
New Conch People who were not born or raised in the Keys but have lived there at least 10 years and are creating community, becoming somewhat local.
Military / veterans Military personnel and families, mostly connected to Naval Air Station.
Service worker People who work directly to support the tourism economy (hotel staff, retail, food service, etc.).
Snowbird People who live in Key West only during the winter.

What do real estate and housing tell us about class in Key West?

U.S. Census Bureau data from 2020 to 2024 show the rising cost of living (Census QuickFacts, World Population Review, EZ Home Search). They help us understand the present-day world of our Twelfth Night and how Key West's communities interact across classes.

  • Who owns: about 49.2% of residents own and live in their homes, which indicates a significant permanent population. The remaining units are vacant or rented out as investments serving the tourism industry, with owners using them only occasionally.
  • The cost: average rent is about $2,220 and the average home value is $852,400. Owning and renting are not affordable for many low-income people, and 10.9% of essential workers live below the poverty line.
  • The commute: the high cost of living forces many workers to live outside Key West and commute from , a working-class neighborhood nearby where housing is less expensive.
  • Multiple jobs: during tourist season, many lower-income people work multiple jobs in the tourism industry, because tourists sustain the island's economy.
  • What it shows: the wealthy can live in, rent, and invest in property to increase and maintain their social status. Many others, including immigrants and long-term residents like the Conch community, can't cover basic living expenses or keep up with the luxury, holiday-focused lifestyle of the tourism industry. The lower-income workers who provide essential services bear the burden, and social mobility remains limited, with only some people able to attain a higher social status.
