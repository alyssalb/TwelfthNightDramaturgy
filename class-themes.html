Social hierarchy in Elizabethan England

What did the hierarchy look like?

  • The clearest division was between gentlemen and non-gentlemen: aristocrats who mainly owned land or whose families had great wealth and power, versus farmers and servants who worked directly for a living. Within that division there were six subdivisions:
    1. Live-in servants, widows, the unemployed, and the aged, dependent on charity.
    2. Laborers: agricultural and urban workers who lived outside their employers' households.
    3. Farmers and tradespeople: , husbandmen, artisans, shopkeepers.
    4. Local gentlemen, usually connected with land ownership.
    5. The county elite: squires, knights, and barons.
    6. Peers: dukes, marquises, earls, viscounts, and barons.
  • There were also those that operated outside of this hierarchy, such as merchants, lawyers, and religious figures, who were seen as outsiders and gained power and influence through money and position. Their social status roughly corresponded to the income pyramid, as Lawrence Stone explains (Stone 21).
  • These standards were shifting, and social standing moved from being based solely on land ownership toward something more complex: money, profession, family background, and social recognition. There were multiple avenues for advancement, but that didn't mean someone's perceived standing automatically matched the group they were trying to join.

Was social mobility possible in the lower classes?

  • Most people were lower class: according to , 90 to 95 percent of the population (Stone 20).
  • Moving up was extremely difficult for groups 1, 2, and 3. Opportunities were limited, and agricultural labor was the main means of survival. Even mobility within their own class, through new economic opportunities or a new occupation, was limited, and moving to a different class was less common. Lower groups could still find some mobility.
  • Mobility depends on how accessible the upper classes were and how much elite classes provide or control advancement. In sponsored mobility, someone in the upper class supports another's rise.
  • In this play, the mobility of desire, whether Malvolio's or anyone else's, is largely determined and controlled by the upper classes.

Class in contemporary Key West

Who lives in Key West today?

  • Information on specific groups is limited but demographic statistics suggest a racially and culturally diverse community, including Cuban, Bahamian, white, Black or African American, and other backgrounds.
  • There are significant economic divisions. Wealthy individuals, investors, and business owners can afford expensive properties and lifestyles, while many of the workers who sustain the island's economy struggle with the high cost of living.
  • Several distinct community groups:
Group Who they are
Conch Long-term inhabitants of Key West, usually with Bahamian and Caribbean lineage dating back to the nineteenth century.
New Conch People who were not born or raised in the Keys but have lived there at least 10 years and are creating community, becoming somewhat local.
Military / veterans Military personnel and families, mostly connected to Naval Air Station.
Service worker People who work directly to support the tourism economy (hotel staff, retail, food service, etc.).
Snowbird People who live in Key West only during the winter.

What do real estate and housing tell us about class in Key West?

U.S. Census Bureau data from 2020 to 2024 show the rising cost of living (Census QuickFacts, World Population Review, EZ Home Search). They help us understand the present-day world of our Twelfth Night and how Key West's communities interact across classes.

  • About 49.2% of residents own and live in their homes, which indicates a significant permanent population. The remaining units are vacant or rented out as investments serving the tourism industry, with owners using them only occasionally.
  • Average rent is about $2,220 and the average home value is $852,400. Owning and renting are not affordable for many low-income people, and 10.9% of essential workers live below the poverty line.
  • The high cost of living forces many workers to live outside Key West and commute from , a working-class neighborhood nearby where housing is less expensive.
  • During tourist season, many lower-income people work multiple jobs in the tourism industry, because tourists sustain the island's economy.
  • All this shows us that the wealthy can live in, rent, and invest in property to increase and maintain their social status. Many others, including immigrants and long-term residents like the Conch community, can't cover basic living expenses or keep up with the luxury, holiday-focused lifestyle of the tourism industry. The lower-income workers who provide essential services bear the burden, and social mobility remains limited, with only some people able to attain a higher social status.
