Queer themes

Disguise, desire, and the island's own history.

Content note: this page uses the word "queer" in a reclaimed context. Please take care when reading if that may upset you.

Twelfth Night is a play that is very easy to give a queer reading to, and that's a lot of what we're going to be doing here. There are quite a few things to go over, so this page is not a full discussion. It's a compilation of research and loosely related thoughts to get everyone on the same page for any discussions we end up having.

Twelfth Night (and Shakespeare) be gay

Queer Shakespeare (as in the person)

  • Shakespeare was probably what we would now consider bisexual or pansexual (here's an article about it).
  • That matters because it raises a question: could any queer subtext in Shakespeare have been, in some way, intentionally placed? Could his queerness have had a tangible impact on his works?
  • Either way, his works have often been reinterpreted or given queer readings, and Twelfth Night is no exception. His sonnets are very much explicitly queer, many of them written to various men.
  • Another article goes into this while also looking at his works (including Twelfth Night!) and giving some historical context.

Queer vs. gay

"Are those not the same thing?" Yes! Well, no.

  • Queer is a word with a heavily loaded history. For the longest time (and in some contexts, even to this day), it was used as a slur against LGBTQ people. The technical "actual meaning," as provided by the ever wonderful Merriam-Webster, is "differing in some way from what is usual or normal."
  • Now it's a word many people use to define themselves (on its own, or as part of an identity such as "genderqueer"), and a catch-all for " or hetero or allo or non-conforming to the norms that these terms are rooted in." In other words, it's used as a synonym for LGBTQ+, and it's often considered the Q in that acronym.
  • A dichotomy has reemerged in recent years between being "queer" and being "gay," or just part of one identity:
    • Being queer is aligning yourself with being part of the community. By understanding the in different LGBTQ experiences, and how they all differ from the norm and often suffer for it, you can take part in community at a time when it is needed. Alternatively, it may be seen as using a slur to define yourself and aligning yourself with groups you may disagree with on values or beliefs.
    • Being "just gay" or "just bi" or "just trans" is, from one side, failing to see how all LGBTQ identities and their oppressions intersect. From the other, it is identifying with the identity that fits you specifically, with no particular desire to interact with a community whose only common ground is a label.
    • People who label themselves "queer" also tend to be more politically active.
  • It's not new. The split was at least old enough to be a dichotomy worth discussing in the 1990s, in the article "The Queer/Gay Assimilationist Split: The Suits vs. the Sluts," which notes versions of it going back to the 1960s and (often considered the start of the modern queer rights movement).
    • Soon after Stonewall, two organizations met: an assimilationist one () and a more radical one ().
    • Later, the GLF itself had a faction split off to focus only on assimilation and legislation regarding gay people: .
    • To grossly oversimplify, it's the "apologist" gay versus the "abolitionist" gay.

Queer vs. gay in theatre

What would be the difference between a "gay show" and a "queer show"?

  • A "gay show" would more likely present gayness from a more assimilationist lens.
    • Maybe it has only one or two queer characters, and a big part of the story is the acceptance of the individual, without looking at societal factors.
    • Maybe it treats homophobia and transphobia as individual failings rather than the societal issue they actually are.
    • It may be less willing to engage with its queer characters in any substance, instead using the aesthetics of queerness, like drag, to develop straight characters or to market the show.
  • A "queer show" may be more willing to engage with the social context of queerness and the lives of queer people, or have something to say about the queer experience itself rather than about one or two gay characters and how they're treated. Maybe it takes an intersectional look at queerness and explores things alongside it, like disability and the experiences of people of color.
  • This is the approach we want to take with Twelfth Night. It would be really easy to just add queer elements. We want to look at the play specifically through a queer lens, playing with gender and what the expectations around it may mean for our characters in the modern day, while looking at what it would have meant for them when the play was written.

Queerness in Shakespeare's time

We're looking at this as best we can with the information we have, from the point of view of England, since that is what Shakespeare would have been most affected by.

  • The condemning "buggery" as a crime punishable by death was the biggest piece of legislation that would have affected him. Buggery is a synonym for sodomy, which generally refers to anal sex (usually targeted against gay men) but can also refer to bestiality.
    • It did result in the deaths of various people, but because it required a witness to prosecute, it was difficult to actually do anything with.
    • It still deepened the stigma against queer people, especially gay men.
    • Interestingly, it was mainly put into action to undermine the authority of the Catholic church in England.
  • Trans history is hard to find: there's very little about people living as trans in the 1500s, let alone specifically in England.
  • Crossdressing is worth mentioning, though: every woman's role in Shakespeare's plays was played by a man. This caused some controversy at the time, but it was by no means illegal, which implies that to some extent crossdressing was legal (and I can't find any evidence to the contrary).

That ending, huh?

With all the discussion of making sure our production focuses on the queer elements, there is one thing that may seem impossible to work around: the ending.

  • What we're working on: actively finding ways to queer the ending, and making sure audiences don't walk away with any theme of queerness being something you can "grow out of," or that you can "find the right man." Or, given that in our interpretation Viola is some form of nonbinary, the ever-dreaded "detransition because you get a boyfriend." Ew and yuck and all the things.

  • What's in the text: one of the biggest pieces is Orsino's speech to Viola at the end (5.1, lines 359 to 362 of our cut):

    We will not part from hence.--Cesario, come, For so you shall be while you are a man. But when in other habits you are seen, Orsino's mistress, and his fancy's queen.

  • Why it matters: he's using her disguised name, Cesario, the name he used when he believed Viola to be a man. Depending on how you read it, that can imply a level of attraction to that side of Viola, to her more masculine side. That's at least one thing we can look at and interpret as we wish.

The Florida Keys as a place for queer people

  • A known safe space. Search "Florida Keys" with some variation of "LGBTQ" and you'll get guides upon guides on gay tourism in the Keys, especially Key West. It's known as a safe space for LGBTQ people, especially those looking to travel somewhere warm.
  • In spite of the state. Florida's government has repeatedly tried to limit efforts to include the LGBTQ community. Most recently, Key West's tourist events, such as its Pride parades, have lost any state funding for 2027 onward. That same article has the executive director of the Key West Business Guild estimating that over 250,000 queer people visit Key West every year.
  • Locally, the other direction. As recently as 2023, the Key West city commission passed a resolution declaring Key West a safe space for queer people (after some drag queens crashed a meeting!). Key West recently elected the first openly out lesbian mayor in Florida, and decades ago elected one of the first openly out queer men to be mayor in the United States.

So why is Key West so gay?

It's difficult to find LGBTQ history specific to Key West. It doesn't seem to have been well documented, or at least it's been hard to find from specifically reputable sources. Here's an article I was able to find, though. The TL;DR:

  • The downturn: the Keys were suffering economically. Laws stopped one of the main economic activities from being profitable or viable (they couldn't plunder shipwrecks for profit anymore), Tampa became the new hub for cigar making, and the temporary funding and attention from the World Wars ceased.
  • The rumor: after the end of WWII, gay circles started spreading word of Key West, an out-of-the-way, hard-to-get-to place, as a great place for queer people (especially gay men) to vacation.
  • 1967: David Wolkowsky, a gay native of Key West, opened a resort, and the rest is history.
  • After that: gay bars opened, gay art galleries opened, more queer people came to vacation (and some to stay and build homes), and the first-ever LGBTQ destination marketing organization in the country opened: the Key West Business Guild. Sound familiar? We mentioned them earlier.
  • Today: Key West (and, more or less, the Florida Keys as a whole) is a sort of safe space for queer people, many of whom are also part of the running of the island, as those two mayors show. A lot of the businesses in Key West are owned by queer people, and often for queer people. It's an extremely queer place.

What does this have to do with our Twelfth Night?

  • The usual version: Viola and Sebastian get shipwrecked. Viola crossdresses for safety. Love shenanigans happen, and everyone is straight in the end.
  • Our version: set it in the Florida Keys, in Key West, and reimagine characters like Olivia and Orsino as business owners there (and, as we've established, businesses in Key West are usually queer-owned), and all of a sudden we have a lot of room to play with.
  • Where we are: we don't have everything nailed down yet, and a lot of it will come from playing with you all as the cast and crew of this show. But we have room to explore and play, and the historical backing to do it. Isn't that what we're here to do?

Gender in Twelfth Night

By no means an exhaustive exploration.

  • Safety and expression: depending on your gender expression, you're more or less safe in certain spaces, and you end up having to work under different rules. That's just a fact, and Twelfth Night acknowledges it. Viola doesn't crossdress for nothing; she needs to fake being a man for her safety.
  • In the modern day, it can read differently. An AFAB (assigned female at birth) person crossdressing now would probably be more dangerous, thanks to the anti-trans rhetoric in the media, especially in a state like Florida. That doesn't really make sense now, so we need to search for a new reason.
  • A new reason: maybe gender exploration. Especially if we read Viola as a foreigner from a place where queerness isn't as allowed, suddenly shipwrecking in a place like Key West may be an excuse to let loose and figure things out.

Gender is a performance (so how do we perform it?)

  • Gender as a construct: while some things are dictated by biological sex (height, the sound of your voice, the reproductive system), most of the things we associate with being a man or a woman were created by people. What says that a dress is feminine? What says that a suit or tie is masculine? These ideas weren't brought into the world by divine intervention. Societies created them over time.
    • High heels used to be worn by rich men, and dresses used to be worn by children of all genders.
    • Many of the behavioral traits we associate with gender are just traits anyone can exhibit, but that we notice more in people of a certain gender expression.
  • It changes, and that's okay. Our concepts of what can be associated with gender change frequently, and right now they're changing constantly rather than over decades or centuries. That doesn't make them any less real. It just means they can change, and that, like many concepts, we should be willing to question them.

  • popularized the idea of gender as a performance: the way you express your gender is a performance. It can get more complicated, but that's the gist. Here's a video of Butler explaining her perspective, and an article on the subject with a quote that stands out:

    "Gender is not passively scripted on the body, and neither is it determined by nature, language, the symbolic, or the overwhelming history of patriarchy. Gender is what is put on, invariably, under constraint, daily and incessantly, with anxiety and pleasure, but if this continuous act is mistaken for a natural or linguistic given, power is relinquished to expand the cultural field bodily through subversive performances of various kinds" (Butler 531).

  • What that means for us: gender is not inherently something that comes from being male or female; it is what we are almost forced to put on. That can be pleasurable, and it can be dysphoric. But assuming it isn't a performance, and not questioning it, gives power to the idea that gender is simply natural.
