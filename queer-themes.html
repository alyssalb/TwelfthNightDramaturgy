Queer themes

Content note: this page uses the word "queer" in a reclaimed context. Please take care when reading if that may upset you.

Twelfth Night as a play has a pretty obvious queer reading, which we will be leaning into for this production. This page gives us a start to a much bigger conversation, and acts as a research and thought starter to get us all on the same page as we start to rehearse and discuss the play and our take on it.

Twelfth Night (and Shakespeare) be gay

Queer Shakespeare

  • Based on speculation, Shakespeare may have been what we would now consider bisexual or pansexual (here's an article about it).
  • We mention this mostly because it raises interesting questions about queer subtext in his work and whether it is intentional within his writing.
  • His works have often been interpreted through a queer lens, in particular Twelfth Night and his sonnets (many of which are written toward a male subject).
  • Another article goes into this while also looking at his works (including Twelfth Night!) and providing some historical context.

Queer vs. gay

"Are those not the same thing?" Yes! Well, no.

  • Queer is a word with a heavily loaded history. For the longest time (and in some contexts, even to this day), it was used as a slur against LGBTQ people. The technical "actual meaning," as stated by Merriam-Webster, is "differing in some way from what is usual or normal."
  • Now it's a word many people use to define themselves (on its own, or as part of an identity such as "genderqueer"), and a catch-all for " or hetero or allo or non-conforming to the norms that these terms are rooted in." In other words, it's used as a synonym for LGBTQ+, and it's often considered the Q in that acronym.
  • A dichotomy has reemerged in recent years between being "queer" and being "gay," or just part of one identity:
    • Being queer is aligning yourself with being part of the community. Alternatively, it may be seen as using a slur to define yourself and aligning yourself with groups you may disagree with on values or beliefs.
    • Being "just gay" or "just bi" or "just trans" is, from one side, failing to see how all LGBTQ identities and their oppressions intersect. From the other, it is identifying with the identity that fits you specifically, with no particular desire to interact with a community whose only common ground is a label.

Queer vs. gay in theatre

What would be the difference between a "gay show" and a "queer show"?

  • A "gay show" would likely present a narrow understanding or depiction of LGBTQIA+ characters, or minimally depict a gay relationship of some sort.
    • It may be less willing to engage with its queer characters in any substance, instead using the aesthetics of queerness, like drag, to develop straight characters or to market the show.
  • A "queer show" may be more willing to engage with the social context of queerness and the lives of queer people, or have something to say about the queer experience itself rather than about one or two gay characters and how they're treated. Maybe it takes an intersectional look at queerness and explores things alongside it, like disability and the experiences of people of color.
  • In Twelfth Night, we are looking to take a specifically queer approach, playing with themes of gender and the expectations around it and what that might mean for our characters in the modern day (as well as when the play was written).

From the Director: Gay theatre is usually kind of moralizing in tone and tells stories that frame the gay experience as one that pretty much just like the heterosexual experience... plays whose thesis or message is basically "don't hate on us, we're just like you!" Usually these stories are about coming out, being marginalized, and they assume the default world to be a homophobic and hetero/cis-normative one. Straightness is the lens, acceptance of gays is the goal.

I have done a few of these types of plays, but what I want this production of Twelfth Night to be is much more aligned with "Queer Theatre"... which doesn't apologize for gender or sexual orientation or compare it to hetero/cis-normativity... it celebrates and embraces our otherness with wild abandon. Queerness in this world is not a problem that needs solving. Queerness is the default lens: authenticity and joy is the goal.

I'd even go so far as to say perhaps Malvolio could be the only fully cis/hetero character in this play, but even his heterosexuality and gender aren't the problem in this queer world... his blind ambition and willingness to sell himself out to climb into aristocracy are.

Queerness in Shakespeare's time

  • In Shakespeare's time, one of the biggest pieces of legislation affecting the community was a condemning "buggery", a crime punishable by death.
    • While this likely resulted in deaths, it required a witness to prosecute and was therefore difficult to uphold.
    • It still, however, deepened the stigma against queer people, especially gay men.
    • Interestingly, it was mainly put into action to undermine the authority of the Catholic church in England.
  • Trans history is hard to find: there's very little about people living as trans in the 1500s, let alone specifically in England.
  • Crossdressing is worth mentioning, though: every woman's role in Shakespeare's plays was played by a man. This occasionally caused controversy, but was legal and in fact endorsed by the state, which did not allow female actors on a stage. Women offstage were also not allowed to crossdress, and were punished for committing such an offense in public (as a "public indecency").

That ending, huh?

With all this in mind as we work to ensure our production aligns with our intentions, we'll have some work to do with the ending when we get there!

  • We are working to find ways to queer the ending, and make sure audiences don't walk away with any theme of queerness being something you can "grow out of," or that you can "find the right man." Or, given that in our interpretation Viola is some form of nonbinary, the ever-dreaded "detransition because you get a boyfriend." Ew and yuck and all the things.

  • One of the biggest pieces of textual support we have in that section is Orsino's speech to Viola at the end (5.1, lines 359 to 362 of our cut):

    We will not part from hence.--Cesario, come, For so you shall be while you are a man. But when in other habits you are seen, Orsino's mistress, and his fancy's queen.

  • Here, Orsino uses Viola's disguised name, Cesario, the name he used when he believed Viola to be a man. Depending on how you read it, that can imply a level of attraction to that side of Viola, to her more masculine side.

The Florida Keys as a place for queer people

  • A known safe space. Search "Florida Keys" with some variation of "LGBTQ" and you'll get guides upon guides on gay tourism in the Keys, especially Key West. It's known as a safe space for LGBTQ people, especially those looking to travel somewhere warm.
  • In spite of the state. Florida's government has repeatedly tried to limit efforts to include the LGBTQ community. Most recently, Key West's tourist events, such as its Pride parades, have lost any state funding for 2027 onward. That same article has the executive director of the Key West Business Guild estimating that over 250,000 queer people visit Key West every year.
  • Locally, the other direction. As recently as 2023, the Key West city commission passed a resolution declaring Key West a safe space for queer people (after some drag queens crashed a meeting!). Key West recently elected the first openly out lesbian mayor in Florida, and decades ago elected one of the first openly out queer men to be mayor in the United States.

So why is Key West so gay?

It's difficult to find LGBTQ history specific to Key West. It doesn't seem to have been well documented, or at least it's been hard to find from specifically reputable sources. Here's an article I was able to find, though. The TL;DR:

  • The Keys were suffering economically. Laws prevented one of the main economic activities (plundering shipwrecks for profit) from being viable or profitable. Tampa became a new hub for cigar making, and the temporary funding and attention from the World Wars ceased.
  • After the end of WWII, it seems that gay communities started spreading word of Key West as a great place for queer people (esp. gay men) to vacation, as it was an out of the way and hard to get to place.
  • In 1967, David Wolkowsky, a gay native of Key West, opened a resort, and the rest is history.
  • Gay bars opened, gay art galleries opened, more queer people came to vacation (and some to stay and build homes), and the first-ever LGBTQ destination marketing organization in the country opened: the Key West Business Guild.
  • Today, Key West (and, more or less, the Florida Keys as a whole) is a sort of safe space for queer people, many of whom are also part of the running of the island, as those two mayors show. Many businesses are owned by/for queer people, and it is known to be a queer-friendly hub.

What does this have to do with our Twelfth Night?

  • In usual interpretations of our play, Viola and Sebastian get shipwrecked. Viola crossdresses for safety. Love shenanigans happen, and everyone is straight in the end.
  • In our version set in the Florida Keys, we reimagine characters and their place in the local environment and find ourselves with a lot of room to play with in characterization and queer interpretations.
  • We anticipate further conversation and exploration in the room, and the dramaturgy team will continue to conduct research as necessary and helpful to facilitate these discussions and discoveries.

Gender in Twelfth Night

By no means an exhaustive exploration.

  • Depending on your gender expression, you're more or less safe in certain spaces and you may end up having to operate under different rules. Twelfth Night in the original text acknowledges this fact! Viola crossdresses in the original context to be safer as a man than as a woman alone in a strange place.
  • In our contemporary interpretation, the reason for Viola's crossdressing has the potential to read differently. In our interpretation, we are interested in exploring Viola's exploration of gender and the excuse of the festival politics and time of revelry in a brand new unfamiliar place to help them learn more about themself.

Gender is a performance (so how do we perform it?)

  • Gender as a construct: while some things are dictated by biological sex (height, the sound of your voice, the reproductive system), most of the things we associate with being a man or a woman were created by people. What says that a dress is feminine? What says that a suit or tie is masculine? When we say gender is a construct, we mean precisely this, that these ideas about gender and expressions of gender were created by societies over time.
    • For example, high heels used to be worn by rich men, and dresses used to be worn by children of all genders.
    • Many of the behavioral traits we associate with gender are just traits anyone can exhibit, but that we notice more in people of a certain gender expression.
  • These perceptions shift over time too. Our concepts of gender associations change frequently, and we perceive more shifts and instability in this perception over time as well. This change is a positive attribute of social constructs and we should be willing to question them and be open to new explorations and discussions.

  • popularized the idea that the way one expresses their gender is itself a performance, or "performative". While this concept is fairly complicated, here is a video of Butler explaining her perspective and an article on the subject. From the article we get a pretty solid description of the concept:

    "Gender is not passively scripted on the body, and neither is it determined by nature, language, the symbolic, or the overwhelming history of patriarchy. Gender is what is put on, invariably, under constraint, daily and incessantly, with anxiety and pleasure, but if this continuous act is mistaken for a natural or linguistic given, power is relinquished to expand the cultural field bodily through subversive performances of various kinds" (Butler 531).

  • For us, this means that gender isn't inherent, it is constructed. The performance or presentation of gender can be pleasurable, or displeasurable. It can be affirmative or dysphoric. It can be neutral, even! But assuming that gender is inherent and not performed renders it unquestionable and provides the concept with (mostly unearned) power without critique, which we would like to avoid.
